# CV Tips: Industry, Research, and International Applications

## First clarify what “CV” means in this context

“CV” does not mean exactly the same thing in every hiring market.

- **Industry applications in the US and Canada:** The expected document is usually a concise resume, even if people casually say CV.
- **UK, Europe, India, and many other markets:** “CV” often means the standard professional application document. For most non-academic roles, it is still concise and role-targeted.
- **Academic and research roles:** A CV may be longer and include publications, presentations, teaching, grants, research experience, and academic service where relevant.
- **Graduate admissions:** Requirements vary by institution and program. Follow the stated format and content requirements rather than a universal page rule.

The job posting, institution, and local convention outrank generic advice. A long academic CV is not automatically stronger for an industry engineering role.

## Think like the evaluator

The first screen is trying to establish eligibility, relevance, evidence, and risk. A strong CV makes the answer easy:

1. Can this person meet the role’s essential requirements?
2. Have they done something close enough to the target work?
3. What was their contribution versus their team’s contribution?
4. Can the claims be checked or discussed in an interview?
5. Are dates, qualifications, and scope clear?

Do not make the reader search through a life history to find the answer.

## Pick the right CV strategy

| Target | Main purpose | Content to prioritize |
|---|---|---|
| AI / applied GenAI | Prove you can build and evaluate useful systems | Projects or work involving retrieval, model/API integration, evaluation, data pipelines, deployment, and limitations |
| Backend / platform | Prove you can design and maintain services | Python, API design, database use, testing, reliability, deployments, observability where true |
| Data engineering / analytics | Prove you can produce trustworthy data and useful outputs | SQL, transformations, validation, pipelines, dashboards, stakeholder use, data definitions |
| Research internship | Prove research readiness and intellectual fit | Research question, methods, technical depth, experiments, writing, relevant coursework, research outputs |
| Master’s / graduate application | Show preparation and direction for the program | Education, relevant projects, research, technical preparation, academic distinctions, carefully selected experience |
| AEC / engineering technology | Show value from both domain and software skills | Engineering domain context, geometry/data workflows, applied AI, collaboration with specialist users |

## Recommended structure for an industry CV

1. **Name and contact:** City/country if useful, email, phone, LinkedIn, GitHub/portfolio. Use clean, working links.
2. **Professional headline or short summary (optional):** Two to three lines tied to the target role. Avoid personality claims such as “dynamic, passionate rockstar.”
3. **Technical skills:** Group skills by category and list only those you can discuss credibly.
4. **Experience:** Reverse chronological; two to five bullets per relevant role as a starting point. Give older or unrelated roles less space.
5. **Selected projects:** Include two to four high-signal projects when they strengthen the target application.
6. **Education:** Degrees, institution, dates, and relevant distinctions/coursework if they help.
7. **Publications, certifications, awards, or volunteering:** Only if relevant and verifiable.

For a research CV, expand the sections that show research ability. For an industry CV, do not add publications, coursework, or every certificate just to make the document longer.

## Academic/research CV structure

A longer CV may include, where applicable:

- Education and thesis/dissertation
- Research interests stated specifically, not as a list of fashionable fields
- Research experience and the candidate’s exact contribution
- Publications, preprints, posters, and presentations with accurate status labels
- Methods, tools, datasets, and experimental techniques
- Teaching, mentoring, and academic service
- Awards, grants, fellowships, and relevant professional service
- Selected technical or engineering projects
- References, only when requested or customary

Never label a draft or planned paper as a published paper. Distinguish “published,” “accepted,” “under review,” “preprint,” and “in preparation” accurately. Do not imply authorship or ownership of a team’s output beyond your contribution.

## How to write bullets that survive a technical interview

Use this pattern:

**Action + technical context + reason/constraint + outcome/evidence.**

Weak: “Worked on machine learning and created dashboards.”

Stronger, if accurate: “Built a Python/SQL workflow to prepare [data type] for [testing or analysis], adding [validation/check] to make [failure or inconsistency] easier to identify.”

Weak: “Used LangGraph, FastAPI, React, Pinecone, OpenAI, Gemini, and many tools.”

Stronger, if accurate: “Implemented a FastAPI endpoint that orchestrated [specific workflow] and returned [defined output]; documented [one limitation or failure mode] and tested [defined cases].”

A number is useful only when it is real and meaningful. If you cannot quantify impact, use concrete scope: the workflow, artifact, test coverage, data type, user group, or design decision.

## Skills section: signal, not inventory

- Put the most relevant skills first for the role.
- Separate **languages**, **frameworks**, **data/AI methods**, **infrastructure**, and **domain knowledge** where it improves readability.
- Do not give yourself proficiency ratings such as 9/10 or “expert” without a defensible standard.
- Do not list a library solely because it appeared in a tutorial.
- Remove skills you cannot explain under follow-up questioning.
- Show critical skills inside project/experience bullets; a skills list alone is weak evidence.

For an AI engineer role, for example, Python, APIs, data handling, evaluation, retrieval, and debugging may be stronger signals than a long list of model names. For a backend role, API contracts, SQL, tests, error handling, and system boundaries may matter more.

## Tailoring without rewriting your whole life

Maintain a **master CV** with all accurate experience, then make a targeted version for each role family. Change the order, emphasis, summary, skills, and selected projects—not the facts.

1. Identify three to five must-have requirements in the role description.
2. Mark where each is proved in your experience or projects.
3. Move the strongest evidence near the top.
4. Use the employer’s terminology when it accurately describes your work.
5. Remove irrelevant details that displace stronger evidence.
6. Ensure the document still represents you honestly.

### A skill-to-evidence map

| Requirement | Weak proof | Stronger proof |
|---|---|---|
| Python | “Python — advanced” | A codebase, service, data workflow, tests, or design decision you can explain |
| RAG / GenAI | Library names | Data ingestion, retrieval design, evaluation method, failure cases, and the part you implemented |
| Backend | “Familiar with FastAPI” | Endpoint contracts, validation, persistence/integration, error handling, and tests |
| SQL / data | “Good SQL skills” | A real transformation, data-quality check, join/aggregation logic, or dashboard workflow |
| Communication | “Excellent communicator” | Documentation, stakeholder-facing dashboard, a technical presentation, or cross-functional example |
| Domain knowledge | Degree title alone | A project where domain constraints influenced a technical decision |

## ATS and formatting

- Prefer a single-column, text-readable structure for general industry applications.
- Use conventional headings such as Experience, Projects, Education, and Skills.
- Avoid text boxes, complex tables, important information in headers/footers, and decorative graphics when ATS parsing is a concern.
- Use a selectable-text PDF unless the employer asks for a DOCX or a portal specifies another format.
- Check extracted text after exporting: copy it into a plain-text editor and inspect reading order.
- Use consistent dates, punctuation, capitalization, and file naming, e.g. `First_Last_AI_Engineer_CV.pdf`.
- Never add hidden keywords, fake skill claims, or a keyword dump.

## Candidate-specific positioning example

For a candidate with mechanical engineering, data analytics, Python/SQL, and applied GenAI exposure, the story should depend on the target:

- **AI/backend:** Lead with implemented Python/API/data/GenAI evidence. Use mechanical engineering as a supporting quantitative foundation.
- **Data roles:** Lead with SQL, data preparation, repeatable pipelines, and dashboards. Include GenAI only if relevant.
- **AEC AI roles:** Bring forward geometry/engineering context and applied AI workflows; show that you can translate domain requirements into software.
- **Research:** Lead with the research question, methods, experimental evidence, and writing—not only product implementation.

Avoid calling yourself “an AI engineer” solely because AI libraries are on the skills list. Let the projects and experience justify the title.

## Common reasons a CV loses credibility

- Responsibilities with no evidence of what changed or what was built.
- A large skills list but no matching examples.
- Inconsistent dates, degree names, or job titles.
- Projects that are described as production systems when they are demos or prototypes.
- Metrics with no baseline, definition, or measurement method.
- Dense paragraphs instead of scannable evidence.
- Overemphasis on unrelated coursework or certificates.
- A personal profile that is generic enough to fit any candidate.

## Final review

Read each bullet and ask: **Could I explain exactly what I did, why I chose that approach, what failed, and how I know it worked?** If not, narrow the claim until the answer is yes.
